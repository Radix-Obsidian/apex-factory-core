## We Built a Machine to Kill Startup Ideas. The First Two It Killed Were Ours.

*Shipping is a solved problem. Making money isn't. Everyone sells you the first thing. Nobody will tell you the truth about the second.*

---

I've watched a specific kind of heartbreak happen over and over, and maybe you've lived it: you ship something. It works. It's even good. You post it, you tell your friends, you check Stripe.

Nothing.

So you ship another thing. And another. Five products in, a wall of green checkmarks behind you, and the total revenue is still a number you can write without a comma. The tools did exactly what they promised. Lovable built it. v0 designed it. Cursor and Bolt and Replit Agent moved you from idea to live URL in an afternoon. Every one of them held up their end.

Here's the uncomfortable thing nobody in that ecosystem will say out loud: **shipping was never your bottleneck.** It hasn't been for a couple of years now. The bottleneck is the question that comes *before* you open the editor — should this thing exist? Will anyone pay for it? Is there already a free, better version of it sitting at the top of a Google search you never ran?

And that question has no vendor. Think about it. Every tool you use is structurally incentivized to help you build *anything*. They make money when you generate, when you deploy, when you keep going. Not one of them has a reason to look at your idea and say "stop — this won't make money, and here's the proof." Their entire business model is the opposite of that sentence.

So we built the thing that says it.

The premise is almost rude in how simple it is: most ideas should die, and the kindest thing you can do for a builder is kill the bad one fast — with receipts — before they spend three weeks of their life on it.

It's a default-to-NO machine. You bring it an idea, and it doesn't cheerlead. It judges the idea against *live* market reality — real named competitors, real evidence of demand or the lack of it — and it tries its hardest to talk you out of building it. Not on vibes, not a generic "have you considered the market?" but on what's actually out there, right now, when you actually look.

In a factory there's a thing called the andon cord. Any worker on the line can pull it to stop everything the instant they spot a defect, because the most expensive thing you can do is keep building on top of a flaw. The whole vibe-coding world handed you a line that runs at full speed and forgot to install the cord.

What I built is the cord.

I know how that sounds. "A service that tells you no" is a hard thing to trust, because of course the incentive could just flip the other way — tell everyone no, look smart, build nothing. So before I asked a single one of you to hand me an idea, I had to prove the machine would do the one thing that actually earns trust: kill something *I* loved.

### So we pointed it at our own flagships. It killed both.

This is the part I'd rather not write, which is exactly why I'm writing it.

We didn't start by judging other people's ideas. That would've been cowardly — easy to be brutal with a stranger's baby. We pointed the line at our own two flagship products. The two things *we* were betting the company on.

The first was an LLM-infrastructure play. Real code, real effort, a thesis we believed in. The verdict: the "unique" pieces at the heart of it were already commodity — the exact capability we thought was our moat was sitting in off-the-shelf open-source libraries anyone could install for free. We didn't have a product. We had a box of parts. Killed. That one stung.

But the second one is the story I actually tell people.

It was a SaaS we'd internally code-named "MCPShield" — AI-tool governance, the kind of thing that sounds important in a pitch deck. We had it roughly 60% built. Two-plus weeks of real work. And the machine killed it not once but twice over:

- The name was *already taken*. There's a live product at mcpshieldai.com. We'd been about to launch with someone else's name.
- There were roughly **25 direct competitors** already in the space — including a toolkit from *Microsoft* that's free and open-source. We were going to charge for a worse version of a thing Microsoft gives away.
- And the killer detail, the one that still makes me wince: the *one* differentiator we kept pitching — a finance-specific vertical, the thing that supposedly made us special — existed in our slide deck and in **zero lines of the actual codebase.** We'd been lying to ourselves and writing the lie into our own marketing.

The machine caught that. It looked at the gap between what we *said* the product was and what the code *actually did*, and it called us on it. It saved us somewhere between eight and ten more days of building a commodity whose name we didn't even own.

I'm not going to pretend that felt good. It felt like getting dumped by something that had read my own deck and my own code and quietly laid the two side by side until the gap was the only thing in the room. That's what you're getting when you hand me your idea. A machine that won't even spare its own creators is the only kind of "no" worth listening to.

### One last thing, plainly.

Let me be straight about where this stands.

I have no shipped product for sale. No paying customers. No revenue. Nothing to "validate." This post is me probing whether the pain I described at the top is real for enough of you to be worth building around. The only proof I have in the world is the two self-kills above. That's it. I'm not going to invent a "we've validated 200 ideas" number or stuff a fake testimonial in here. The entire premise is that I won't lie to you.

So here's the offer, exactly as it works:

**1. Bring me your idea. I'll kill it — for free.** A brutal verdict, judged against live market data: real named competitors, real demand evidence, the gap between what you *think* you're building and what's actually out there. If your idea is fragile, you'll know in days instead of finding out in three weeks of wasted nights. That alone is worth the email.

**2. If it survives — and most won't — I'll build and ship it *with* you.** For the first few survivors I'll do the build for free, because I want the case studies more than I want your money right now. I need proof this works on someone other than me. You get a co-builder; I get a story to tell. After those first few, it becomes a paid thing. That's the honest arc of it.

The valuable half is the first half. Everyone on earth will help you ship. I'm offering to be the one person who tells you *not* to — and proves it before you waste the time.

If you've shipped five things and made zero dollars, the problem was never your hands. It was the question nobody made you answer first.

I'll answer it for free, and I'll start with no and mean it.
